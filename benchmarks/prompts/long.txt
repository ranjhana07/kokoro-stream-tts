The river valley has been farmed for more than 3,000 years, and the terraces on the eastern slope are older than any of the villages that now depend on them. Each spring the families climb to the upper fields at around 5:30 am, before the heat rises, and repair the stone walls that the winter rains have loosened. By the 20th of April the first channels are open and water runs down from the glacier fed lake, one terrace at a time, for about 14 km.

Visitors usually arrive in late summer, when the barley is almost ready. Guides charge $25 for a half day walk, and most groups stop at the small shrine near the 2nd bridge to rest. The oldest resident, Dr. Mehta, a retired surveyor, still keeps a notebook of the water levels that goes back to 1974. He says the pattern has shifted by roughly 8% in his lifetime, and he is careful to add that his numbers are only as good as his eyes.

In the evening the whole valley smells of woodsmoke and boiled milk. Children carry lanterns to the lower road, where the bus from the town stops for exactly two minutes. If you plan to stay overnight, book early, because there are only 9 rooms in the village and the season is short.
